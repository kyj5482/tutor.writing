# Reading ladder — what to read next, and why

*Curated 2026-07-25 from everything they've actually read and written about.*

These are **not** a required list. The point is that when a kid finishes a book at 9pm and
asks "what now?", there's a real answer waiting instead of a shrug — and that the answer is
chosen from what they already love, not from a generic grade-level list.

**How the lanes work:**

- 🟢 **Same lane** — they will almost certainly like it. Safe, fast, keeps the streak alive.
- 🔵 **One step sideways** — same pleasure, new flavor. This is how a reader's taste widens.
- 🟣 **One step up** — a stretch in difficulty or subject. Offer, never assign.

# Jia (Grade 6)

**What she's read:** *The Land of Stories* · **Harry Potter 3 → 7 — the whole series finished
2026-08-06**, ~3,400 pages between 06-09 and 08-06 · **📗 *The Honest Truth* (Dan Gemeinhart),
finished 2026-08-17.** Immersive series reader with real stamina — she read a 766-page book and
started the next one the same day.

> ⭐ **Update 2026-08-17 — she picks for herself now, and she picked *away* from her comfort.**
> This list existed largely to solve the post-Potter moment. She solved it unaided: during an
> 11-day gap she found *The Honest Truth* — realistic, contemporary, no magic at all, a boy with
> cancer running away to climb a mountain with his dog — and finished it. **So stop treating her
> as a fantasy reader who needs the next fantasy handed to her.** The 🟢 Same lane picks below are
> still good, but they are no longer the obvious answer. Ask her what she wants *after a book like
> this one*, and put at least one realistic/contemporary title on every menu from now on.
>
> **If she liked *The Honest Truth*, the natural next three** (offer as a pick-one menu, one line each):
> **Wonder** (R.J. Palacio) — a boy the world stares at, told by everyone around him too ·
> **Fish in a Tree** (Lynda Mullaly Hunt) — a girl who has hidden that she can't read ·
> **Bridge to Terabithia** (Katherine Paterson) — friendship, and what it costs. All three run on
> the thing her writing keeps going to: **what a hard thing costs the person it happens to.**

**What she actually likes** (from her own writing): friendship and loyalty · a character
being treated unfairly · secrets and backstory · magic systems with rules.

### 🟢 Same lane

| Book | Author | Why her |
|------|--------|---------|
| **Percy Jackson: The Lightning Thief** | Rick Riordan | The classic next-after-Potter. Hidden world, misfit kid, found family — funnier and faster than HP. If she only takes one, take this. |
| **Nevermoor: The Trials of Morrigan Crow** | Jessica Townsend | A cursed girl, a magical city, a competition to join a secret society. The closest thing to the *feeling* of first reading Harry Potter. |
| **Amari and the Night Brothers** | B.B. Alston | Missing brother, secret magical bureau, entrance trials. Same shape, modern voice. |
| **Keeper of the Lost Cities** | Shannon Messenger | 💡 **Her brother is reading this.** A book you can argue about with someone in your own house is worth two books. |

### 🔵 One step sideways

| Book | Author | Why her |
|------|--------|---------|
| **When You Trap a Tiger** | Tae Keller | 🌟 **Top pick.** Korean folklore, a grandmother, a girl who makes a bargain with a tiger. Newbery Medal. Magic she knows how to read, in a world she already knows — and it will hit differently than Hogwarts does. |
| **The Girl Who Drank the Moon** | Kelly Barnhill | Newbery. A witch, a swamp monster, a baby fed on moonlight. Beautiful sentences — good for a reader whose next skill is quoting. |
| **A Wrinkle in Time** | Madeleine L'Engle | Quieter and stranger than what she's used to. Meg is angry and awkward and saves everyone *with* those traits. |
| **Aru Shah and the End of Time** | Roshani Chokshi | Hindu mythology, two girls, a lot of jokes. Same engine as Percy Jackson, completely different world. |

### 🟣 One step up

| Book | Author | Why her |
|------|--------|---------|
| **When You Reach Me** | Rebecca Stead | Newbery. A mystery that only assembles in the last ten pages. Will teach her more about *structure* than any lesson can. |
| **Front Desk** | Kelly Yang | Realistic, funny, a kid running a motel front desk. **She has read almost no realistic fiction** — this is the gentlest door into it. |
| **Wonder** | R.J. Palacio | Multiple narrators telling one story. Also: she writes best about unfairness, and this book is entirely about it. |

### ⚠️ One honest note about Jia's reading

She has read **fantasy series, almost exclusively, for two months.** That's not a problem —
that's a reader with a taste. But every book she's written about has magic in it, which means
she has never had to write about a real person doing an ordinary hard thing.

The 🟣 lane is there for that reason. Offer, don't push. *"Finish Half-Blood Prince first"* is
always the right answer if she asks.

# Jaei (Grade 8)

**What he's read:** *The Scorch Trials* · *Everblaze* & *Neverseen* (Keeper of the Lost
Cities) · four *39 Clues* books back-to-back · *Storm Warning*. Reads for pace and puzzle;
sets a book down the moment it stops pulling.

**What he actually likes** (from his own writing): twists · betrayals · who is really on
whose side · clues that click together · and — unusually — **how the author built it**. He
has commented unprompted on POV shifts, pacing, and an author's signature phrasing.

### 🎯 The author bridge nobody has told him yet

He just finished ***Storm Warning*** — **The 39 Clues #9, by Linda Sue Park.**

Linda Sue Park is a **Korean-American author who won the Newbery Medal**. He read her without
knowing it. That is the single best recommendation opening available to him:

> *"You just finished a book by a Newbery Medal winner and didn't know it. Want to see what
> she writes when it's her own story?"*

| Book | Author | Why him |
|------|--------|---------|
| **A Single Shard** | Linda Sue Park | 🌟 Newbery Medal. 12th-century Korea, an orphan and a master potter. Short, quiet, nothing like *39 Clues* — and by an author he already trusts. |
| **Prairie Lotus** | Linda Sue Park | A half-Korean girl in the 1880s American West. Same author, again completely different. |

### 🟢 Same lane

| Book | Author | Why him |
|------|--------|---------|
| **Holes** | Louis Sachar | 🌟 **Top pick.** Three timelines, a hundred-year-old curse, and every scattered clue locks together at the end. Built for a reader who likes watching the machine work. |
| **The Westing Game** | Ellen Raskin | Newbery. Sixteen heirs, a puzzle in a will, and the reader is genuinely invited to solve it first. The purest *39 Clues* pleasure ever written. |
| **The Hunger Games** | Suzanne Collins | The natural step after *The Scorch Trials* — same engineered-experiment premise, but far more interested in who's watching. |
| **Artemis Fowl** | Eoin Colfer | A 12-year-old criminal mastermind runs a heist against the fairy world. All plot, all scheme. |

### 🔵 One step sideways

| Book | Author | Why him |
|------|--------|---------|
| **The Giver** | Lois Lowry | Short, and the ending is an argument. He's a natural arguer — this will give him something to be furious about. |
| **Refugee** | Alan Gratz | Three kids, three eras, three escapes — braided like *Holes*, and they converge. Real history, thriller pacing. |
| **Bomb: The Race to Build the World's Most Dangerous Weapon** | Steve Sheinkin | **Nonfiction that reads like a heist thriller** — spies, sabotage, a countdown. He has read no nonfiction; this is the way in. |

### 🟣 One step up

| Book | Author | Why him |
|------|--------|---------|
| **Ender's Game** | Orson Scott Card | Strategy, manipulation, a kid who realizes the adults are running an experiment on him. Directly in his wheelhouse, a real step up in difficulty. |
| **Scythe** | Neal Shusterman | Dystopia with a genuinely hard moral question at the center. Ideal for a reader who writes counterarguments for fun. |

### ⚠️ One honest note about Jaei's reading

He sets books down (*Neverseen*, twice). **Let him.** Six finished books in five weeks is a
lot of reading; a reader who abandons a book that stopped working is a healthy reader, not a
quitter. Never make finishing a book a condition for anything.

His real gap is **range** — everything he reads is a fast-paced adventure series. The Linda
Sue Park bridge is the most natural way in, because the trust is already there.

# 👫 Buddy read — both of them, same book

They earned the 🤝 **Double Trouble** badge for writing on the same day. A shared book is the
next level of that: same chapters, same night, two completely different entries — and a real
conversation at dinner that isn't about homework.

**Recommendation: *When You Trap a Tiger* by Tae Keller.**
Newbery Medal, Korean folklore, works at both a 6th- and 8th-grade level, and it is short
enough that neither of them stalls. Jia gets the magic and the sisters; Jaei gets a structure
worth taking apart.

**Alternate: *Refugee* by Alan Gratz** — three braided storylines, which would give Jaei
something to analyze and Jia three characters to care about.

**How to run it:** same chapters, both write the same evening, different templates (say, one
does 03 Character Diary and the other does 04 Opinion), then read each other's entries. Award
🤝 Double Trouble as normal.

## Notes for whoever maintains this list

- **Never recommend a book that's currently an example book** in `examples/` to a student who
  is about to read it — a student reading *Holes* while being shown *Holes* examples every
  session is being handed sentences to copy. Swap the example set instead.
- Re-curate at each weekly if a student finished a book or abandoned one.
- Recommendations here don't need to be books the tutor can quote accurately — that bar
  applies to `examples/`, not to this list. Here, a good match matters more than perfect recall.
- Keep the ⚠️ honest notes. A reading list that only tells parents good news isn't worth much.
