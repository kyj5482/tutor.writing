## Tutor feedback

🌟 **Two things you did well:**

1. **You took the "could have written X, instead Y" move and scaled it up to the size of a series.** I showed you that move an hour ago on a six-word sentence — *"There is no lake at Camp Green Lake."* Sentence level. You applied it to **book architecture**: Messenger *could have resolved the parents in Lodestar and written a completely different book six.* That is a much harder version of the same technique, because you have to hold two whole books in your head to see the alternative. Copying a move is normal. Enlarging it is not.

2. **The brackets.** You wrote *"[Sophie's] parents were taken by the Neverseen."* Square brackets are how professional critics mark a word they changed **inside** a quotation so it fits their own sentence — the original almost certainly said *"your parents"* or *"my parents"*, and you adjusted it and told the reader you adjusted it. That is the exact opposite of the thing on your Watch list. You didn't just avoid putting your own words in quotation marks — you did the version that shows precisely where the author stops and you start. Watch streak **0/3 → 1/3**, first draft, no flag from me.

🔧 **One thing to level up — and it's tomorrow's first job, not a repair.**

Your last clause is your **verdict**, and right now it argues with itself:

> *"a book with many conflicts with no build-up stories **which will shorten the amount of action**"*

If Messenger cuts the build-up and opens mid-emergency, the reader gets **more** action, sooner — not less. So one of two things is true and I genuinely don't know which you meant:

- **(a)** it shortens the **build-up**, so the action starts earlier — that's a *gain*, or
- **(b)** conflict with no build-up makes the action **matter less**, because you haven't been made to care yet — that's a *cost*.

(b) is the more interesting claim and it is a real criticism. (a) is praise. **The day-2 version needs that sentence to commit to one.** That's the THE TRADE box, and it's your verdict, so it has to be unmistakable.

🚀 **Stretch challenge (+10 XP, optional): name the technique in the opening line.**

Compare the two claims:

- Yours: *"Messenger chooses to make a difficult situation for Sophie and her friends."*
- Sachar's: *"Sachar tells you how to read the entire novel in the seventh sentence."*

Yours describes the **situation**. His names the **decision**. Rewrite your first sentence so it names what Messenger *did as a writer* — the shape is *"Messenger opens ______ instead of ______."*

And here's the gift, because you found this technique on your own without knowing it has a name: starting a story already inside the crisis, with the setup withheld, is called ***in medias res*** — Latin, "into the middle of things." It's roughly three thousand years old; it's how the *Iliad* opens. You identified it in chapter 1 of a middle-grade fantasy by noticing what the author *didn't* do. That's the job.
