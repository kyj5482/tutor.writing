## My writing

In the Keeper of the Lost Cities, the author uses in media res by just dropping the readers in the room with another Mr. Forkle, instead of building up the story to get there.

In the first chapter of the Nightfall, Messenger opens to make a difficult situation for Sophie and her friends. They just figure out that "Sophie's parents were taken by the Neverseen", instead of writing the story on the last book to finish this conflict and could have wrote another story for this book, which would have nothing different in the big picture. But, however, Messenger chose to start the "Nightfall" with the conflict. which shows us that this will be a book on many conflicts and no build-up stories which will shorten the amount of action.

In the eleventh chapter of the "Nightfall", when she just learned that "her family was taken", the author suddenly makes Sophie and her friend "come inside the lighthouse" and then make her meet Mr. Forkle's twin brother.

This method as a reader myself is kinda frustrating, but it's worth it because it gives me more "Ahh" moment. This frustration helps built the concentration and more of a completion.

🌟 **Two things you did well:**

1. **The claim names the technique now, and it lands on a short sentence.** Yesterday: *"Messenger chooses to make a difficult situation for Sophie."* Today: *"the author uses in media res by just dropping the readers in the room with another Mr. Forkle, **instead of building up the story to get there.**"* Two separate things happened there. The claim moved from naming the **situation** to naming the **decision** — that's the line between a reader and a critic, and it was the whole 🔧 from day 1. And the "instead of" clause is *short*, after a long one. Long, then short. I flagged that rhythm twice (07-29 on the Alistair sentence, 07-31 when both paragraphs collapsed into one) and today it happened without me naming it in the moment.

2. **You ran the Two-Source Test on yourself and it cut both ways.** Five pairs of quotation marks. You dropped the two that were yours (*"uses in media res"*, *"Mr. Forkle's twin brother"*), you kept the two you checked against the book, and then you did the harder half — you **put the marks back on the Sophie citation you'd deleted.** That's the part most people never get. Removing quotation marks isn't the skill. The skill is one question, asked in both directions: *whose words are these?* Hers → quote them. Mine → say them plainly. **Watch item 3/3 → retired.** 🧹

🔧 **One thing to level up — ¶3 is missing its last box.**

Your third paragraph does THE PROOF and stops:

> *"...the author suddenly makes Sophie and her friend come inside the lighthouse and then make her meet Mr. Forkle's twin brother."*

That's what Messenger **did**. It never says what it **does to you**. Compare the *Hunger Games* example — it proves the present tense, and then: *"Twenty-four children are going to die and the reader has no guarantee about the twenty-fifth."* That sentence is the effect, and it's the reason the proof was worth quoting.

You already know the answer here, because you said it in ¶4 — *frustrating*. So ¶3 needs one line: **what does it feel like to sit in a chapter where a second Forkle exists and nobody will explain him?** One sentence. That's the missing box.

🚀 **Stretch challenge (+10 XP, optional): land ¶4 on a short sentence.**

Your verdict is *right* — cost, payoff, judgment, all three. But it arrives in one long breath and then trails off. Look at where the *Hunger Games* analysis ends:

> *"A book about a nation forced to watch children die in real time should not be narrated by someone comfortable enough to use the past tense. **The form is the argument.**"*

Five words. That's what a landing sounds like. Keep your sentence about frustration and the "Ahh" — then add one **short** sentence after it that says what the frustration is *for*. Not a summary of the paragraph. A verdict.
