# 14 — Craft Analysis

**Skill:** reading like a writer — how the author built it, not what happened
**Best when:** something in the writing *worked on you* and you noticed
**Ladder stage:** 🔬 Critic (stage 6)

---

## The shift this template asks for

Every other template asks: **what happened, and what do you think about it?**

This one asks a different question:

> **The author made a choice here. What did that choice cost, and what did it buy?**

Books don't just *happen*. Somebody decided to put that scene there, in that order, in that
person's voice. When you can name the decision, you're not a reader anymore — you're a
critic. And critics get paid.

---

## Techniques worth hunting for

You don't need fancy words. You need to notice the choice.

| Choice the author made | What to ask |
|---|---|
| **Point of view** — whose head are we in? | What do we *not* get to see because of it? |
| **Order** — why is this scene here, not earlier? | What does holding it back do to me? |
| **What's withheld** — info the author refuses to give | How does not-knowing feel? |
| **Word choice** — one strange, specific word | Why *that* word and not the ordinary one? |
| **Dialogue vs. summary** — heard, or reported? | Why let us hear this line out loud? |
| **Sentence length** — short. Choppy. Or long and rolling. | What does the rhythm do? |
| **What repeats** | Why say it three times? |
| **How a chapter ends** | Where exactly did the author cut? |

---

## The shape

```
THE CLAIM     — name the technique: "Collins keeps cutting to the audience."
   ↓
THE PROOF     — quote the exact place it happens
   ↓
THE EFFECT    — what it does to the reader (that's you — say what it did to YOU)
   ↓
THE TRADE     — what it costs, and why it's worth it anyway
```

That last box is what separates Tier 3 from everything below. **Every technique costs
something.** Say what.

---

## Tier 1 — the frame (~150 words)

- I noticed that the author ______ (names the choice).
- You can see it here: "______" (the quote).
- This makes the reader ______.
- I think the author did it on purpose because ______.

✅ Checklist: names a *choice*, not an event · one exact quote as proof · says what it does
to the reader.

---

## Tier 2 — a real analysis (~250 words, 3–4 paragraphs)

- **Open with the technique**, stated as a claim about the author:
  *"Sachar hides his most important information in a story that seems unrelated."*
- **Prove it with two examples** from different parts of the book, each with a short quote.
- **Explain the effect** in your own words — not "it makes it interesting," but what it
  specifically does: makes you distrust a character, makes you feel time slowing, makes you
  reread page one.
- **Compare it to the ordinary version.** *"The author could have just said ______.
  Instead they ______."* This is the fastest way to prove a choice was deliberate.

✅ Checklist: a claim about the AUTHOR · 2 quotes from different places · the "could have
said X, instead did Y" move · effect described specifically.

---

## Tier 3 — criticism (350+ words)

Everything in Tier 2, plus:

- **Name the cost.** *"He trades pacing for the payoff — the middle sags, because a reader
  who has solved it is impatient with a character who hasn't."* A technique with no cost is
  a technique you haven't looked at hard enough.
- **Then say whether it was worth it, and why.** That's your verdict, and it's yours.
- **Connect the technique to the book's meaning.** The choice isn't decoration. Why does
  *this* book, about *this* idea, need *this* technique?
- **Quote precisely and briefly.** Three well-chosen words beat a whole paragraph.

✅ Checklist: names the cost · gives a verdict · links technique → meaning · quotes are
short and exact.

---

## If you get stuck

The fastest way in: **find one sentence that made you feel something, and ask why that
sentence and not a normal one.**

> *"There is no lake at Camp Green Lake."*
>
> The author could have written *"Camp Green Lake was a dried-up lake bed in the Texas
> desert."* Same information. Instead he wrote a flat little contradiction and put it in
> the first paragraph — which teaches you, before you meet a single character, that names
> in this book are lies and you should check every one of them.

That's the whole move. One sentence, one question, one answer.
