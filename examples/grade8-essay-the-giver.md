# Grade 8 Example — 12 Essay, second book: *The Giver*

The second **12 Essay** example for grade 8, so a student whose ladder goal keeps landing
them on this template doesn't see the same book every week. Pair with `grade8-growth.md`
(*Holes*) and alternate.

Same reader as `grade8-the-giver.md`, one chapter further on: an 8th grader who has read
**chapters 1–5** — Jonas's community controls jobs, families and feelings; the Ceremony of
Twelve is coming; his father has brought home a struggling newchild, Gabriel; an apple once
seemed to *change* in midair (ch. 3); rule-breakers are "released"; and the pill for the
**Stirrings** arrives in chapter 5, which is why this essay can use it and the shorter

## 12 — Essay *(Tier 3 · built over 3 days)*

**The community in *The Giver* doesn't control people with force. It controls them with
vocabulary, and by chapter four Lowry has shown us the whole machine.**

Start with the word **"release."** It is used for the old, for newchildren who don't
thrive, and for people who break the rules three times, and nobody in the community ever
asks what it actually means — Larissa describes one as if she were describing a retirement
party. A word doing that much work is not a word; it is a lid. Lowry never has to tell us
the community is hiding something, because she lets us watch a whole society use one
comfortable term for three completely different events and notice nothing.

The same trick runs through everything else the community names. Feelings get filed away
each evening at the telling-of-feelings, where Lily's anger is talked through and disposed
of in minutes; the word for what Jonas feels before the Ceremony has to be **"apprehensive"**
rather than "frightened," because precision of language is a rule; even growing up is
handled by a pill the moment the **"Stirrings"** begin. Each of those names sounds careful
and even kind. What each one actually does is end a conversation before it starts — which
is why the apple that *changes* in midair frightens Jonas so much. It is the first thing
he has ever seen that the community has no word for.

That is the argument these first chapters are quietly building, and it explains a choice
that looks strange at first: there is no villain in this book. No dictator gives a speech,
no soldier stands in a doorway. Lowry took the villain out on purpose, because a reader who
is looking for someone to blame will not notice the thing actually doing the damage — the
language everybody in the community, Jonas included, speaks without ever hearing it.

*(≈290 words · Day 1 = thesis + the "release" paragraph · Day 2 = the second paragraph ·
Day 3 = the ending, plus the read-aloud pass.)*

---

### What to steal from this one

1. **The thesis names a technique, not a plot event.** *"Controls them with vocabulary"* is
   a claim about how the author built the book — the 🔬 Critic move — and everything after
   it exists to prove that one sentence.
2. **The evidence is quoted at word level.** You don't need a long passage. **"release,"**
   **"apprehensive,"** **"Stirrings"** — three words in quotation marks, each followed by
   what it costs the people who say it. Short quotes, fully explained, beat long quotes
   dropped in whole.
3. **The last paragraph answers a question the essay raised.** *Why is there no villain?*
   The close doesn't restate the thesis; it uses it to explain a choice you'd otherwise
   miss. A closing you could delete is a restatement, not an ending.
