# Grade 6 Examples — the Growth Templates (12–15)

These demonstrate the four templates that climb the top of the 🪜 Writing Ladder.
They use *Percy Jackson and the Lightning Thief* (chapters 1–5) and, for the comparison,
*Hatchet* — the same books as `grade6-percy-jackson.md` and `grade6-hatchet.md`, so a
student can flip back and see the shorter versions of the same thinking.

**Written at Tier 1–2**, deliberately. These are supposed to look reachable, because they
are. Every one of them was built **one paragraph a day**.

> 👀 Look at the length and don't panic: the essay below is 3 days of normal-sized writing
> stacked together. You already write this much — just not in a row.

---

## 12 — Essay *(Tier 2 · built over 3 days)*

**Percy's real problem is not monsters — it's that every adult in his life has decided he
can't handle the truth.**

The first reason I think this is that Percy spends twelve years believing something is
wrong with *him*. He gets kicked out of six schools, he can't read properly, and he can't
explain why strange things keep happening around him. When his mom won't answer questions
about his father, she says, **"I can't, Percy. I'm sorry."** She is being kind, and she is
also leaving him alone with it. Percy fills the silence with the only explanation a kid
has: that he is the problem.

The second reason is the Mrs. Dodds fight. Percy watches a teacher grow wings and attack
him, and afterward, when he asks Grover about her, Grover just says, **"Who?"** Even the
school records have been changed. This is worse than the monster was. A monster you can
hit with a sword — you can't fight everybody in the room telling you it didn't happen. That
scene is where Percy stops trusting his own memory, and it's the adults who did that to him,
not the Fury.

So when Percy finally reaches Camp Half-Blood, the biggest thing that changes isn't that he
gets a sword. It's that someone finally answers him. That's why the book spends five whole
chapters on being confused before it spends one on being a hero — Riordan wants us to feel
how long Percy waited.

*(280 words · Day 1 = thesis + paragraph 1 · Day 2 = paragraph 2 · Day 3 = the ending + fixes)*

## 13 — Debate *(Tier 2)*

**The question: was Percy's mom right to hide the truth from him?**

I think she was right, even though it hurt him.

My reason is that the moment Percy learns who he is, the monsters can find him. Grover
explains that a demigod's scent gets stronger as they understand themselves, and the proof
is what happens the second Percy starts asking real questions — a Minotaur is at the car
that same night. Sally wasn't protecting his feelings. She was hiding his smell.

**The strongest argument against me is this:** Percy spent twelve years believing he was
stupid and broken, and that is a real injury, not a small one. He says himself that he
figured he was "a troubled kid" — because six schools and every adult told him so. Sally
could have told him he was different *without* telling him he was a demigod. She chose
silence, and Percy paid for that silence every single day at school while the monsters were
nowhere in sight.

That's true, and it's the part of my own argument I like least. But here's what it can't
explain: half-truths don't work on a kid like Percy. Tell him he's special and *can't know
why*, and he does what he does in every chapter of this book — he goes and finds out. Sally
knew her son. A partial answer would have gotten him killed faster than a total lie did.

*(255 words)*

## 14 — Craft Analysis *(Tier 2)*

**Riordan starts the book by having Percy argue with us before we've said anything.**

The very first line is: **"Look, I didn't want to be a half-blood."** Then, a few lines
later, he warns us to stop reading — *"If you're a normal kid… close this book right now."*

The author could have started the ordinary way: *"My name is Percy Jackson, and I'm twelve
years old."* Same information, and completely dead. Instead he starts with the word
**"Look"** — which is a word you only use on somebody who doesn't believe you.

That one word does two things at once. It makes Percy sound tired instead of excited, which
tells you immediately that being special has been bad news so far. And it puts us in the
position of the doubters — all those teachers and adults who didn't believe him — before we
have even met one. By page one we already know what it feels like to be the person Percy is
talking to.

Riordan does it again with the fake warning. Telling a kid to close the book is the single
most reliable way to make them keep reading, and he knows it. It's a joke, but it's also
doing real work: it makes the story feel dangerous and true, like something being handed
over instead of performed.

*(240 words)*

## 15 — Comparative Essay *(Tier 1–2 · built over 4 days)*

**Both *Hatchet* and *The Lightning Thief* strand a boy with no adults to help him. But
Paulsen thinks you survive by becoming someone new, and Riordan thinks you survive by
finding out who you already were.**

In *Hatchet*, Brian has to change or die. Nothing he already knows is any use — his city
life gives him nothing, and the book is a long list of things he gets wrong before he gets
them right. The hatchet his mother gave him is the perfect symbol of this: it's an
embarrassing present in the city, and it's the only useful object he owns in the woods.
Brian's old self is not helpful. He has to build a new one, mistake by mistake.

In *The Lightning Thief*, the opposite happens. Percy doesn't learn anything new about
himself at Camp Half-Blood — he finds out that everything that was already "wrong" with him
was the answer. His dyslexia is Ancient Greek. His trouble sitting still is battle reflexes.
**This is different because** Brian's rescue comes from what he *builds*, and Percy's comes
from what he already *was* and nobody would explain to him.

Reading both made me notice something I don't think I'd have seen in either one alone: both
books are about a kid being handed a lie by the adult world. Brian's lie is that he's safe.
Percy's lie is that he's ordinary. And in both books, the wilderness — real or magic — is
the first place anybody tells them the truth.

*(285 words · Day 1 = question + thesis · Day 2 = Hatchet · Day 3 = Percy · Day 4 = ending)*
