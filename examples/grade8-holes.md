# Grade 8 Examples — *Holes* by Louis Sachar

All examples below imagine an 8th grader who read **chapters 1–15**: Stanley Yelnats IV is
convicted of stealing a pair of sneakers that literally fell out of the sky onto his head,
and is sent to Camp Green Lake — a juvenile detention camp on a lake bed that dried up over
a hundred years ago. Every boy must dig one hole a day, five feet deep and five feet across,
"to build character." Stanley's family blames all its misfortune on a curse from his
"no-good-dirty-rotten-pig-stealing-great-great-grandfather," and the book keeps cutting away
to that story, and to a third story about a schoolteacher named Katherine Barlow, a hundred
years ago, when there really was a lake.

Examples are written at **Tier 2–3**. They're strong but human — your writing should sound
like YOU, not like these.

**👀 Notice in every example:** when someone speaks, you **hear them** — the exact words, in
quotation marks — instead of being told what they said. Watch for it especially in 03, 06
and 10.

---

## 01 — Summary

In the first fifteen chapters of *Holes*, Louis Sachar sets up three stories at once and
makes you wait to find out they're the same story. Stanley Yelnats is convicted of stealing
Clyde Livingston's sneakers — shoes that fell on his head from an overpass — and chooses
Camp Green Lake over jail, mostly because he has never been to a camp. What he finds is a
dried lake bed where every boy digs a hole five feet deep before the sun gets high, and
where Mr. Sir warns him on the first day, "You are not in the Girl Scouts anymore."
Meanwhile, the narrator keeps interrupting to tell two other stories: how Stanley's
great-great-grandfather broke a promise to a woman named Madame Zeroni, and how a
schoolteacher named Katherine Barlow lived here when the lake still had water. Stanley digs,
gets the nickname Caveman, and finds a gold tube stamped KB — which he hands to X-Ray
instead of the guards. What matters about these chapters is that the reader is being handed
three separate puzzles and quietly promised they will click together.

## 02 — Prediction

I have two predictions, and I trust the second one more. What I *expect* is that the gold
tube stamped "KB" belonged to Katherine Barlow, and that the Warden is not making the boys
dig for character at all — she is using them to search for something buried. My evidence is
that the boys are told to report anything "interesting," which is a strange instruction if
the digging itself is the point, and the chapter about Katherine Barlow arrives immediately
after the tube is found. Sachar's pattern so far is that nothing is a coincidence: shoes fall
from the sky, a boy named Zero is at a camp founded on a broken promise to a woman named
*Zeroni*. An author who plants a name that carefully is not writing toward a coincidence. So
my stronger prediction is that Zero and Stanley are connected by the curse, and that one of
them will have to carry the other — literally — before this ends.

## 03 — Character Diary

They give you a shovel and tell you it's a measuring stick. That's the first lie here, and
nobody bothers to hide it.

My hole today was five feet across and five feet down and I still can't tell you what it was
for. Mr. Pendanski keeps calling me Stanley in that voice, like the name is a favor he's
doing me. The other boys call me Caveman, which I hate, except that I don't, because it means
they decided I'm one of them.

I found something. A little gold tube, flattened, with the letters KB on the bottom. I stood
there in my hole holding it and I thought about a day off, and a shower with hot water, and
then X-Ray said, "Hey Caveman, you find anything?" and I heard my own voice say, "No." Then
I gave it to him anyway, the next morning, so he could be the one to turn it in.

I don't know why I did that. I told myself it was because he's been here longest. That isn't
the reason. The reason is that this is the first time in my life anybody wanted something I
had, and I wasn't going to waste it.

Great-great-grandfather, if you're listening: I hope the curse is real. Because if it isn't,
then all of this is just bad luck, and bad luck doesn't owe you an ending.
— Stanley

## 04 — Opinion / Response

Camp Green Lake is not a rehabilitation program, it is unpaid labor with a slogan on top, and
the camp's own motto gives it away. The adults repeat the idea that "if you take a bad boy
and make him dig a hole every day in the hot sun, it will turn him into a good boy," which
sounds like a philosophy until you notice what it lets them skip. Nobody at this camp ever
asks a boy what he did, or whether he did it — Stanley is innocent, and no adult has once
inquired. The digging is presented as the cure, so the crime never has to be discussed.

That is the real function of the motto: it converts a question about justice into a question
about sweat. And the moment the boys are told to report anything "interesting" they find, the
disguise slips, because character-building does not require a search. Some might argue the
camp does produce real change — Stanley is getting stronger, and for the first time he has a
place among boys who don't treat him as a target. That's true, and it's worth conceding. But
it happened *between* the boys, not because of the holes. The camp didn't build that; it just
happened to be where it occurred.

## 05 — Letter to a Character

Dear Stanley,
I want to talk about the moment you gave X-Ray the gold tube, because I don't think you were
being weak, and I think you're telling yourself you were.

You called it "seniority" in your head. But you'd been at that camp long enough to know that
what you were really buying was a place to stand. I don't blame you for that. Everyone reads
that scene as you getting used, and I read it as the first strategic thing you've ever done.

Two questions. Do you actually believe in the curse, or is it easier to be cursed than to be
a kid that unlucky for no reason? And when you said "No" to X-Ray in the hole — did you
decide that, or did you just hear yourself say it?

One more thing, and I'll be careful here because I know something you don't. Pay attention
to Zero. Not to what he does. To his name.
Your reader, Jaei

## 06 — Rewrite the Scene (What If…?)

In the book, Stanley lies to X-Ray about finding the gold tube and then hands it over the
next morning. But what if Zero had been close enough to see him do it?

Stanley's fingers closed around the tube just as X-Ray's shovel stopped scraping.
"Hey, Caveman. You find anything?"
The sun was directly overhead. Stanley could feel the metal edge biting into his palm.
"No," he said. "Just a rock."
"Yeah." X-Ray went back to digging. "That's all that's ever down there."
Stanley climbed out of his hole and there was Zero, ten feet away, standing perfectly still
in a hole he had finished an hour ago. He was looking right at Stanley's closed hand.
Stanley froze. "I was going to —"
Zero shook his head, once. Then he bent down, picked up his shovel, and started widening a
hole that was already wide enough.
He didn't say anything. He didn't say anything for the rest of the day, and that was worse
than if he had.

## 07 — Golden Line

**My golden line:** "There is no lake at Camp Green Lake." (chapter 1)

In the story, this comes in the opening paragraph, before we meet a single character. I think
Sachar puts it there because it teaches the reader how to read the whole book in one sentence:
names in this novel are lies, and you should check every one of them. Green Lake has no lake
and nothing green. Camp is not a camp. Mr. Sir is not a sir. Zero is not nothing.

What makes the sentence work is how flat it is. It doesn't say "shockingly, the lake was
gone" — it just states it, like a fact off a sign, which makes the whole place feel official
and wrong at the same time. That flatness is the book's actual voice, and it's how Sachar
gets away with putting a broken promise, a murder, and a curse into a book that sounds calm
the entire time.

## 08 — Connection

In the book, Stanley's whole family explains their bad luck with the curse — every time
something goes wrong, someone says "I blame it on his no-good-dirty-rotten-pig-stealing-
great-great-grandfather," and everyone laughs, and then nobody has to feel responsible for
anything.

This connects to how my family talks about a business my grandfather lost. The story has been
told so many times that it comes out the same way every time, with the same villain in it,
and I noticed a couple of years ago that nobody in the story ever makes a decision — things
just happen *to* them.

They're alike because both families use a story to convert bad luck into a plot, and a plot
is easier to live inside than randomness. They're different because Stanley's curse turns out
to be *real*, which the reader isn't supposed to accept yet. That's the part I keep thinking
about: Sachar built a book where the excuse is true. It makes me suspicious of how comforting
the curse is to the Yelnats family — they'd been using it as a joke for a hundred years before
anybody had to actually pay it off.

## 09 — Quick Write 3-2-1 *(for a short-reading day)*

**3 things that happened:**
1. Stanley found a flattened gold tube stamped KB at the bottom of his hole.
2. He lied to X-Ray about finding it, then handed it over the next morning so X-Ray could
   turn it in and get the day off.
3. The Warden arrived immediately — which no one expected for a "found object."

**2 words:** "Seniority" — Stanley uses it to justify giving X-Ray the tube. I think it means
rank earned by time served, and it's doing a lot of work here as an excuse. "Interesting" —
Mr. Pendanski's word for what they're supposed to report. It's deliberately vague, and vague
instructions usually mean the person giving them doesn't want to say what they're after.

**1 question:** Why does the Warden care about a lipstick tube? My guess is that she already
knows what's buried out there and roughly where, and the boys are a search grid.

**So what?** The most important thing today was Stanley's lie, not the tube. It's the first
time he chose to be part of the group instead of being protected by an adult, and it's the
first decision he makes in the entire book that isn't made for him.

## 10 — Book Review *(written as if the whole book was finished)*

Can a hundred-year-old broken promise still be owed? *Holes* by Louis Sachar follows Stanley
Yelnats, convicted of a theft he didn't commit and sentenced to dig one hole a day at a
detention camp on a dry lake bed — while two other stories, a century apart, keep interrupting
his.

The best thing about this book is the construction. Sachar runs three timelines and never
explains how they connect; he just sets pieces down and walks away, and by the end every
single one has a job. A pair of sneakers falls from the sky in chapter three and doesn't get
explained until the last thirty pages, and when it does, it's not a coincidence — it's the
answer. The dialogue is doing quiet work too. When Mr. Sir says, "You are not in the Girl
Scouts anymore," he tells you everything about the camp's idea of discipline in nine words,
and Sachar never has to describe the place's cruelty directly.

One thing that could be better is that the Kate Barlow chapters slow the middle down, and the
first time through I was impatient to get back to Stanley. I'd give it 4.5 out of 5. It loses
half a star for that middle drag — but honestly, it earns a full 5 on a reread, because those
chapters stop being interruptions once you know what they're for, which might be the point.
If you liked *The 39 Clues* for the puzzle-solving, read this: it's the same pleasure of
watching scattered clues lock together, except Sachar hides his in plain sight.

## 11 — ACE Write (Bonus) *(tutor asked: "Why does Sachar tell three stories instead of one?")*

**A —** Sachar splits the book into three timelines so the reader solves the curse before
Stanley does, which turns a survival story into a mystery the reader is complicit in.

**C —** The narrator interrupts Stanley's first days at camp to explain Elya Yelnats and
Madame Zeroni, and then again to open the Katherine Barlow story — and drops the detail that
Elya was supposed to *carry Madame Zeroni up the mountain* and never did. The book states the
unpaid debt plainly, a hundred pages before Stanley has any reason to think about it.

**E —** This matters because it changes what suspense means in this book. We aren't waiting to
find out what happened; we're waiting for Stanley to catch up to what we already know. Every
time he blames "bad luck," the reader can see the actual mechanism, and that gap is what makes
the ending land — when Stanley finally carries Zero up the mountain, we recognize it a beat
before he does. On the other hand, this structure costs Sachar something real: the middle
sags, because a reader who has solved it is impatient with a character who hasn't. He trades
pacing for the payoff, and I think it was worth it.
