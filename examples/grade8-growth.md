# Grade 8 Examples — the Growth Templates (12–15)

These demonstrate the four templates that climb the top of the 🪜 Writing Ladder, using
*Holes* and *The Hunger Games* — the same books as `grade8-holes.md` and
`grade8-the-hunger-games.md`, so a student can flip back and see the shorter versions of
the same thinking.

**Written at Tier 2–3.** They're strong but human — yours should sound like YOU.

> 👀 Every one of these was built **one paragraph a day**. The essay below is three normal
> sessions; the comparison is four. Nothing here was written in a single sitting, and
> nothing here needs to be.

---

## 12 — Essay *(Tier 3 · built over 3 days)*

**Camp Green Lake is not a rehabilitation program. It is unpaid labor with a slogan on
top — and the camp's own motto is what gives it away.**

The adults repeat the idea that if you take a bad boy and make him dig a hole every day in
the hot sun, it will turn him into a good boy. It sounds like a philosophy right up until
you notice what it lets everyone skip. Nobody at Camp Green Lake ever asks a boy what he
did, or whether he did it. Stanley is innocent — the sneakers fell out of the sky onto his
head — and in fifteen chapters not one adult inquires. The motto is doing that work
deliberately: it converts a question about justice into a question about sweat, and sweat is
much easier to supervise.

The disguise slips completely the moment the boys are told to report anything
**"interesting"** they find. Character does not require a search. If digging is the cure,
it should not matter what comes out of the ground — and yet the Warden arrives within
minutes of a flattened gold tube surfacing. The holes were never the point. The boys are a
search grid, and the slogan is what keeps them from noticing.

Some might argue the camp does produce real change, and that's fair — Stanley gets stronger,
and for the first time in his life he has a place among boys who don't treat him as a
target. I'd concede that completely. But that happened *between the boys*, in the space the
adults weren't looking at. The camp didn't build it; the camp just happened to be where it
occurred. Which is the quiet, furious thing Sachar is saying: the only rehabilitation at
Camp Green Lake is the part nobody planned.

## 13 — Debate *(Tier 3)*

**The question: did digging holes change Stanley — or did Zero?**

I think it was Zero, and I think Sachar structured the whole book to make you argue about it.

My reason is that Stanley's real change is a *decision*, not a strength. The first genuinely
free choice he makes in the entire novel is teaching Zero to read — nobody orders it,
nobody rewards it, and it costs him digging time he can't spare. Everything before that
happened *to* him: the sneakers fell on him, the judge sentenced him, the shovel was handed
to him. Zero is the first thing Stanley chooses.

**The strongest argument against me is that the digging demonstrably worked**, and I have to
take it seriously. Stanley arrives at Camp Green Lake soft, friendless and passive, and he
leaves it able to carry another human being up a mountain. That isn't a metaphor; the book
makes it literal. The holes built the body that carried Zero, and Sachar spends chapter
after chapter on blisters and sunrise and the exact dimensions of a hole precisely so we
feel that transformation in our arms. Without the digging, the ending is physically
impossible.

That's true. But I think it answers a different question than the one that matters. The
digging gave Stanley the *capacity* to carry Zero; it gave him no reason to. Camp Green Lake
runs on the idea that every boy is out for himself, and a hundred years of Yelnats history
runs on a debt nobody paid. What breaks that is not muscle — it's Stanley deciding a
friend's name is worth more than a day off. The Warden could have produced the arms. Only
Zero could produce the reason.

What would change my mind: if Stanley had carried Zero up the mountain in chapter three,
before the holes, and still made it. Then the digging would be scenery. It isn't — I just
don't think it's the cause.

## 14 — Craft Analysis *(Tier 3)*

**Sachar tells you how to read the entire novel in the seventh sentence, and then dares you
to remember it 200 pages later.**

The line is: **"There is no lake at Camp Green Lake."** It arrives in the opening
paragraph, before a single character, stated flatly, like something printed on a sign.

The author could easily have written *"Camp Green Lake was a dried-up lake bed in the
Texas desert, abandoned for over a hundred years."* Identical information, and completely
inert. Instead he wrote a small contradiction and refused to explain it — which teaches the
reader, before the story has started, that **names in this book are lies and you are
expected to check every one of them.** Green Lake has no lake and nothing green. Mr. Sir is
not a sir. Camp is not a camp. And Zero, the boy everybody treats as nothing, is a Zeroni —
the name the entire hundred-year curse is hanging from.

What makes it work is the flatness. A dramatic version (*"shockingly, the lake had
vanished"*) would announce itself as a clue, and clues that announce themselves get solved
early. Sachar's version sounds bored. It slips past you as scene-setting, and it is
actually the instruction manual.

The cost is real, though, and worth naming: this technique only pays on a reread. First time
through, that sentence does nothing but describe weather, and a reader who never returns
never collects. Sachar is spending his opening paragraph — the most valuable real estate in
a novel — on an effect most readers won't feel until the second pass. I think it's worth it,
because it's the same bet the whole book makes: three timelines that mean nothing until they
mean everything. The first sentence isn't just teaching you to read *this* page. It's
training you for the structure of the entire novel.

## 15 — Comparative Essay *(Tier 3 · built over 4 days)*

**Both *Holes* and *The Hunger Games* hand a child a system that claims to be fair. The
difference is who each book asks to look away — and Collins makes that person the reader.**

Sachar hides the unfairness inside a slogan. Camp Green Lake insists that digging "turns a
bad boy into a good boy," and the sentence is designed to be repeatable — an adult can say
it without thinking, and a boy can be worked all day underneath it. The people looking away
in *Holes* are entirely inside the book: the judge, Mr. Pendanski, everyone who never asks
whether Stanley did it. We readers are on the outside, and Sachar puts us there
deliberately. We are given the Kate Barlow chapters and the Elya Yelnats chapters so that we
know more than Stanley does. Our position is comfortable. We get to be the only honest
person in the room.

Collins refuses to give us that seat. The unfairness in Panem isn't hidden in a slogan; it's
broadcast, sponsored, and scored. **"Happy Hunger Games! And may the odds be ever in your
favor!"** is said out loud, warmly, to a square full of terrified children — nobody is being
fooled, including us. And then the arena arrives and it is genuinely thrilling, and Collins
keeps cutting to the sponsors and the cameras and the audience until you notice that the
audience is you. Where Sachar gives the reader the moral high ground, Collins takes it away
and makes the taking the point.

This comparison strains in one place, and the strain is worth admitting: Sachar is writing
comedy and Collins is writing a war novel, so of course one implicates its reader more than
the other. But the strain is what exposes the mechanism. Both books know that an unfair
system survives on people not looking. They just disagree about where the not-looking
happens — for Sachar it's a failure inside the story, and for Collins it's a habit outside
it.

If I have to choose, Collins does the harder thing. It costs her something real: *Holes* is
a more pleasurable book, and part of that pleasure is exactly the comfort she withholds. But
a book that lets you watch children fight and never asks what you're doing there has told
you about injustice, while *The Hunger Games* has made you practice it. The question I can't
answer is whether a reader can feel that and still enjoy the arena — or whether enjoying it
is the proof (Collins, chs. 1–5; Sachar, chs. 1–15).
