# Grade 6 Example — 13 Debate, on *Hatchet*

*A second Debate example, on a different book from the one in `grade6-growth.md`.*

**Why this file exists:** `grade6-growth.md` was the only Debate example in the library, and
it's *Percy Jackson* — which meant that on any day Percy Jackson had just been shown, the
Debate template had no legal example left under the rotation rule. Same gap that
`grade8-craft-analysis-hunger-games.md` fixes for grade 8. When a template becomes part of a
student's chosen ladder path, it needs a second book.

**Pair it with:** books where a character is **alone and carrying something they didn't tell
anyone** — secrets, promises, being cut off from help. Follows a loud fantasy well: *Percy
Jackson* is a big bright world, this is one boy and a lake.

**Written at Tier 2.** It's strong but human — yours should sound like YOU.

> 👀 This was built over **two normal sessions** — the question and your side on day 1, the
> other side and your answer on day 2. Nothing here was written in one sitting.
>
> ✅ Every quotation in it is a real line from the book. That is the rule for this library and
> it is also the rule for your entry: if you can't find the exact words, say it in your own
> words with **no quotation marks** — that's not cheating, that's just honest.

## 13 — Debate *(Tier 2 · built over 2 days)*

**The question: was Brian right to keep the Secret from his father?**

I think he was right, even though it ate at him the whole book.

My reason is that Brian never actually had a good option, only two bad ones. What he saw was
his mother in a station wagon with a man who was not his father, and the book won't even name
it out loud — it just calls it **"The Secret"**, with capital letters, like a thing too heavy
to say. Telling his father would not have unbroken his family. His parents were already
divorcing. It would only have moved the hurt from one person onto two.

**The strongest argument against me is this:** the silence is exactly what left him with
nobody. Brian is by himself in that plane long before it crashes. When the pilot dies, the
book says **"He was alone. In the roaring plane with no pilot he was alone. Alone."** — three
times, in three sentences, because one wasn't enough. A thirteen-year-old was carrying his
family's worst thing without a single person to carry it with, and that is its own kind of
crash. If he had told his father, at least one adult would have known what he was holding.

That's true, and it's the part of my argument I like least. But here's what it can't explain:
Brian doesn't want *company* for the Secret, he wants it to not be true. Telling wouldn't have
given him that. What actually helps him is the lake — where nobody can be told anything, and
he stops waiting for someone to fix it and starts building a fire. He needed the silence to
run out on its own.

*(268 words)*

## The moves to point at when you show this

1. **The question is a real question.** *"Was Brian right to keep the Secret?"* has two
   answers a reasonable person could pick. *"Was the plane crash bad?"* does not.
2. **The other side gets its own paragraph, its own reason, and its own quote** — and it is
   genuinely convincing. If the reader finishes that paragraph thinking *"…actually, yeah,"*
   the paragraph did its job. Paragraph 3 is the one that makes this stage 5.
3. **It admits what it costs:** *"that's the part of my argument I like least."* Saying so out
   loud makes the writer more trustworthy, not less.
4. **The answer is specific, not dismissive.** Not "but that's wrong" — instead, one thing the
   other side **can't explain**. That's the whole move.
5. **Both quotes are short and exact**, and each one sits inside a sentence that says who said
   it or where it happens. A quote dropped in alone doesn't prove anything.
