# Grade 8 Examples — *The Hunger Games* by Suzanne Collins

All examples below imagine an 8th grader who read **chapters 1–5**: Katniss Everdeen, 16,
lives in District 12, the poorest district of Panem. Her father died in a mine explosion, her
mother shut down afterward, and Katniss has kept her little sister Prim alive by hunting
illegally in the woods with her friend Gale. Every year the Capitol holds a reaping: two
children from each district are chosen to fight to the death on live television. Prim's name
is drawn — her very first year — and Katniss volunteers to take her place. The boy tribute is
Peeta Mellark, the baker's son, who once threw her burnt bread when her family was starving.
They are taken to the Capitol, handed a drunk mentor named Haymitch, and dressed by a stylist
named Cinna who sets their costumes on fire.

Examples are written at **Tier 2–3**. They're strong but human — your writing should sound
like YOU, not like these.

**👀 Notice in every example:** when someone speaks, you **hear them** — the exact words, in
quotation marks — instead of being told what they said. Watch for it especially in 03, 06
and 07.

---

## 01 — Summary

In the first five chapters of *The Hunger Games*, Suzanne Collins spends most of her time
showing us what Katniss is willing to do to keep her family alive, so that the volunteering
scene doesn't need explaining. Katniss slips under the fence to hunt illegally with Gale, and
the woods are the only place in the book where she sounds relaxed. Then the reaping arrives,
and Effie Trinket delivers the Capitol's line — "Happy Hunger Games! And may the odds be ever
in your favor!" — in the same cheerful voice she'd use to open a fair. When Prim's name comes
out of the bowl, Katniss shouts, "I volunteer! I volunteer as tribute!" and the district
answers not with applause but with silence and a three-finger salute. Finally, the male
tribute turns out to be Peeta Mellark, the boy who once threw her bread when she was starving,
which Katniss immediately recognizes as a debt she now has to kill to repay. What matters
about these chapters is that Collins establishes the moral trap before the arena — Katniss is
already losing something no matter what she does.

## 02 — Prediction

I have two predictions. What the story seems to *promise* is that Katniss will win by being
the best hunter in the arena. What Collins is actually setting up, I think, is that Katniss
will win by being watched — that the audience, not the weapons, decides who survives. My
evidence is how much page space goes to things that have nothing to do with combat: Cinna
designing a costume to make her memorable, Haymitch's advice about being liked, and the
constant reminder that sponsors send gifts. An author who spends three chapters on
*presentation* before a single fight is telling you where the real contest is.

Collins's pattern so far is to show the cruelty and the entertainment in the same shot — the
reaping is a horror and a broadcast at once. So I don't think she is writing toward a clean
victory. My stronger prediction is that Katniss will do something for the cameras that she
means sincerely, and that the Capitol will punish her for it precisely because the audience
believed it.

## 03 — Character Diary

I have never written anything down before. Writing things down is how they find you.

But tonight there's a bowl of hot food in front of me that nobody worked for, and a bed, and
someone whose whole job is my hair, so apparently this is a night for doing things I've never
done.

They keep asking me how I felt when I volunteered. I didn't feel anything. That's what nobody
wants to hear. Prim's name came out and my body went down the aisle and my mouth said, "I
volunteer! I volunteer as tribute!" and by the time I understood what I'd done it was already
finished. People think courage feels like something. It feels like nothing. It feels like
watching yourself from the back of the crowd.

Then they read the boy's name and it was Peeta Mellark, and I thought: of course it is.

Because of the bread. Because I was eleven and starving in the rain behind his mother's
bakery, and he burned two loaves on purpose and took the beating for it and threw them to me.
I have never once thanked him. I crossed the schoolyard to avoid him for five years rather
than say four words.

And now the Capitol has arranged it so that the only way I go home is if he doesn't.

They should have picked anyone else.
— Katniss

## 04 — Opinion / Response

Katniss's decision to volunteer is usually read as the bravest moment in the book, but I
think Collins wrote it as the least free one, and the text supports that. Katniss doesn't
deliberate. She describes her own volunteering as something that happens before she can think
about it, and the scene is over in a few lines — which is a strange choice for the emotional
climax of five chapters unless Collins wants us to notice the absence of a decision.

This matters because of what the Capitol has actually built. The reaping is designed so that
love is the trap: the more people you care about, the more names you take out, the more of
your own slips you add for extra food. Gale has forty-two entries because he feeds a family.
Katniss volunteers because Prim exists. The system doesn't need to force anyone — it just has
to know who you love and then wait.

Some might argue this makes Katniss less heroic, and I understand the objection: choosing
freely is what makes a sacrifice mean something. But I'd say the opposite. A person who has to
weigh whether to save their sister has a choice. Katniss has a reflex, and the Capitol
installed it. That's a bleaker and more interesting thing for a book to say, and it's why the
silence in the square lands harder than cheering would have.

## 05 — Letter to a Character

Dear Katniss,
I want to talk about the bread, because I think you've told yourself the wrong story about it
for five years.

You describe it as a debt. You say you owe Peeta, and now you can't pay him, and that's what
makes this unbearable. But he didn't lend you anything. He burned that bread on purpose and
his mother hit him for it, and then he threw it to you and never once mentioned it — not at
school, not in the square, not on the train. Nobody who is keeping score does that.

You've turned a gift into a debt because a debt is something you can control, and I don't
blame you for that. Everything else in your life has a price on it.

Two questions. Would you have volunteered for anyone but Prim — and does the honest answer
frighten you? And when Peeta says he wants to die "as himself," do you understand what he
means, or do you just think it's a strategy?

One more thing, and I'll be careful, because I know something you don't. The cameras are not
your enemy. Learn that faster than I did.
Your reader, Jaei

## 06 — Rewrite the Scene (What If…?)

In the book, Katniss never speaks to Peeta about the bread — she avoids it for five years and
carries it into the arena. But what if she had said it out loud on the train?

The Capitol countryside was going past too fast to look at. Peeta had been standing at the
window long enough that Katniss knew he was waiting for her to leave.
"The bread," she said.
He didn't turn around. "I wondered if you were going to bring that up."
"You burned it on purpose."
"I dropped it," Peeta said. "In the fire. Accidentally. Twice."
"Your mother hit you."
"My mother hits me on Tuesdays." He finally looked at her, and he wasn't angry, which was
somehow worse. "You've been crossing the schoolyard for five years, Katniss. I noticed. I
figured you'd say something eventually, and then I figured you wouldn't, and then I stopped
figuring."
"I didn't know what to say."
"You could have said thanks." He shrugged, and turned back to the window. "It's a little late
now. One of us is going to have to kill the other one by Friday."
Behind them, Effie was calling that dinner was served, in the voice she used for good news.

## 07 — Golden Line

**My golden line:** "Happy Hunger Games! And may the odds be ever in your favor!" — Effie
Trinket (chapter 1)

In the story, Effie says this to a square full of terrified children moments before two of
them are chosen to die. I think Collins wrote the line this way because the horror of Panem
isn't cruelty — it's *manners*. A villain who screamed would be easier to read. Effie is
cheerful, and she means it, and that's what makes the scene sit wrong in your stomach.

The word doing the damage is "Happy." Not "welcome," not "let the games begin" — *happy*,
attached to an event where children die on camera. Collins puts a party word and a death
word inside the same phrase and then has a woman in a wig deliver it warmly. That's the whole
Capitol in six syllables.

And "the odds" is the second trick. It reframes a rigged system as luck, which lets everyone
in the square pretend that what's about to happen is weather instead of policy. It makes me
wonder whether Effie knows what she's saying, or whether the Capitol's real achievement is
raising people who can say it and hear nothing.

## 08 — Connection

In the book, Katniss can only relax on the *illegal* side of the fence. The woods are where
she describes herself as almost happy, and District 12 — her actual home, with her actual
family — is where she keeps her face still and says as little as possible.

This connects to something I've noticed about being on a team versus being in class. In a
game I'll talk, argue, tell someone they're wrong, take the shot. In class I'll sit on an
answer I'm confident about because saying it means being looked at. It took me a while to
realize it's not about confidence — it's about which room lets you be judged for what you do
instead of for who you are.

They're alike because both Katniss and I are more ourselves in the place with clearer rules
and higher physical stakes. They're different in a way I don't want to flatten: my classroom
isn't dangerous, and hers is. Katniss's silence in District 12 is a survival strategy in a
place where a wrong word gets you whipped in the square.

What that gave me was a better read on her personality. When people call Katniss cold, I think
they're mistaking a strategy for a temperament — the woods scenes prove she isn't cold, she's
*careful*, and Collins put them first precisely so we'd know the difference before the Capitol
starts filming her.

## 09 — Quick Write 3-2-1 *(for a short-reading day)*

**3 things that happened:**
1. Katniss hunted with Gale beyond the fence on the morning of the reaping.
2. Prim's name was drawn in her first year, and Katniss volunteered in her place.
3. Peeta Mellark was chosen as the male tribute — the boy who gave her bread five years ago.

**2 words:** "Tribute" — the Capitol's word for a child sent to die. It's borrowed from taxes
and offerings, which frames the whole thing as a payment districts owe rather than a
punishment inflicted on them. "Reaping" — a harvest word, used for children. Both words do
the same job: they make an atrocity sound like an agricultural routine.

**1 question:** Why does District 12 respond with silence and the three-finger salute instead
of applause? My guess is that it's the only protest available that the Capitol can't punish,
because nothing was technically done.

**So what?** The most important moment today wasn't the volunteering — it was the salute. It's
the first evidence that the districts aren't fully broken, and it's aimed at Katniss before
she's done anything, which tells me the book is going to be about what a symbol costs the
person who becomes one.

## 10 — Book Review *(written as if the whole book was finished)*

What does it cost to become a symbol you never volunteered to be? *The Hunger Games* by
Suzanne Collins follows Katniss Everdeen, who takes her twelve-year-old sister's place in a
televised fight to the death and then discovers that surviving the arena is the easier half of
the problem.

The best thing about this book is that Collins never lets you enjoy the violence without also
watching yourself enjoy it. The arena is genuinely thrilling — and the book keeps cutting to
the audience, the sponsors, the cameras, until you notice that you are one more person watching
children fight for entertainment. That's a hard trick to pull off without lecturing, and she
does it in first-person present tense, which keeps you locked inside Katniss's head with no
room to look away.

One thing that could be better is the romance. The love triangle is the least convincing part
of a very convincing book, partly because Katniss is written as someone with no interior access
to her own feelings — which is great for war and bad for romance. I'd give it 4.5 out of 5. It
loses half a star there, but earns everything else for the ending, where the victory is
immediately reframed as a political problem instead of a happy one. If you liked *The Scorch
Trials*, read this — same "kids inside an engineered experiment" premise, but Collins is much
more interested in *who is watching the experiment* than Dashner is.

## 11 — ACE Write (Bonus) *(tutor asked: "Why does Collins put the bread memory before the arena?")*

**A —** Collins plants the bread memory early so that Peeta is already a debt before he is
ever an opponent, which makes the arena a moral problem for Katniss instead of a physical one.

**C —** The memory arrives right after his name is called: Katniss, eleven and starving in the
rain behind the bakery, and Peeta deliberately burning two loaves and taking a beating from his
mother to throw them to her. Her own reaction to seeing him on the stage isn't fear of his
strength — it's the recognition that she owes him and can never repay it now.

**E —** This matters because it decides what kind of story the arena will be. If Peeta were a
stranger, the Hunger Games would be a survival plot: outlast twenty-three people. By making him
the person who once kept her alive, Collins guarantees that every strategic choice Katniss
makes is also a betrayal, and that winning and losing stop being opposites. On the other hand,
it does cost her something — the bread scene is so effective that Peeta spends much of the book
as a debt rather than a person, and it takes until the cave scenes for him to become someone
with wants of his own.
