# Example library

Every template, demonstrated on real books. The student sees one of these **before** they
write, so they know what the finished thing looks like.

> **Why that last one is split out.** A student who sets 🔬 **Critic** as their ladder goal
> writes template 14 most weeks, and for a while `grade8-growth.md` was the *only* Craft
> Analysis example in the library — so rule 1 below (never the same book twice running) had
> nowhere to go, and *Holes* got shown three times in five sessions. When a template starts
> repeating because it's someone's goal, the fix is a **second book for that template**, not
> a fourth showing of the first one. Same pattern applies to 12/13/15 if a goal lands there.

These deliberately reuse books the student has already seen shorter examples of, so they can
flip back and see the same thinking at journal length and at essay length. **Each example
notes the day-by-day build** it came from — show that line. A 340-word essay looks
impossible until you see it labelled "three normal sessions."

## Rules for picking one

1. **Never use the same example book two sessions in a row.** This is the whole reason the
   library exists — one book every day, forever, made the examples feel like wallpaper and
   the students stopped reading them.
2. **Pair it to what they're reading today.** If Jia is deep in *Half-Blood Prince*, Percy
   Jackson shows her a hidden-world book doing the same move her book is doing. If Jaei just
   finished a *39 Clues*, *Holes* shows him what a clue-puzzle looks like when the author is
   playing at the top level.
3. **Rotate flavor, not just title.** Three sessions of hidden-world fantasy in a row is
   still monotony even with three different books. Follow a loud book with a quiet one.
4. **Say which book the example comes from, and why you picked it today.** One sentence:
   *"This one's from Holes — same kind of clue-hunting as your 39 Clues, so watch how he
   handles the quote."* It turns the example into a recommendation, which is how a reader
   finds their next book.
5. **The example is a demo, not a target.** Always follow it with: *"Now it's your turn —
   about YOUR book."* Say out loud that the examples are good, not perfect.

## What each set deliberately models

The new books were written to demonstrate the move each student is working on right now.
Point at it when you show the example:

- **Grade 6 sets** put the quote in the **main writing**, never saved for the bonus round —
  Jia's current focus. Every single example has quotation marks in the body.
- **Grade 8 sets** let characters **speak in their own words** instead of reporting what
  they said — Jaei's current focus. Templates 03, 06 and 07 show it most clearly.

When a student's focus skill changes, check whether the example set still demonstrates it.
If it doesn't, that's a sign the library needs another book — not that the student needs
another correction.

## Adding a book

Copy the structure of any existing file: a header paragraph saying exactly how much of the
book the imaginary student read, a note about the tier, then all 11 templates in order
(01–10 plus 11 ACE Write).

Two hard rules:

- **Only add books you can get factually right.** These are teaching materials — a wrong
  character name or invented quote teaches the student something false about a real book.
  Books you half-remember belong in `library/reading-ladder.md` as recommendations, not here
  as examples.
- **Write them at Tier 2 for grade 6 and Tier 2–3 for grade 8**, and mark any example
  written a tier higher. Examples that are too polished stop being reachable.
