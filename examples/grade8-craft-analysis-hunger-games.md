## 14 — Craft Analysis *(Tier 3 · built over 2 days)*

**Collins wrote *The Hunger Games* in the present tense, which means the book has no
survivor telling it.**

Almost every novel is narrated by someone who already made it to the end. Past tense is a
promise: *somebody lived to write this down.* Collins refuses to make that promise. Her first
sentence is **"When I wake up, the other side of the bed is cold"** — *is*, not *was* — and
the entire novel stays there, one second behind Katniss and never one second ahead. Twenty-four
children are going to die and the reader has no guarantee about the twenty-fifth.

The choice does more than build suspense, though, and the place it does the most damage is the
reaping. Prim's name is called, and Katniss's next words are **"I volunteer as tribute!"** — we
get the shout *before* we get the decision, because in present tense there is no gap in which a
narrator could explain herself. A past-tense Katniss would have written *I don't remember
deciding; I remember hearing my own voice.* That version is more beautiful and it is much less
frightening, because it has been thought about. Collins gives us the raw order of events
instead: sound first, meaning later, if ever.

That "if ever" is the real payoff, and it lasts the whole trilogy. Katniss cannot tell you
whether she loves Peeta or is performing for the cameras, and readers argue about it because
**she** doesn't know — a past-tense narrator would have had years to work it out before
speaking. The technique makes her honest and useless at the same time.

The cost is genuine. Present tense strips out reflection, so the book cannot pause to be wise
about what it's showing; when Rue dies, the novel gets grief but not perspective. Collins buys
immediacy and pays in insight, and there are chapters where you can feel the missing older
voice. I think the trade is right anyway, because the subject is a **live broadcast**. A book
about a nation forced to watch children die in real time should not be narrated by someone
comfortable enough to use the past tense. The form is the argument.

*(345 words)*

## The moves to point at when you show this

1. **The claim names a decision, not a situation.** Not *"the book is tense"* — *"Collins
   wrote it in present tense, which means the book has no survivor telling it."* A reader
   describes the situation; a critic names what the author **did**.
2. **Two proofs, from two different places** — the first sentence, and the reaping. Both
   quotes are **short**: five words and four words. Three well-chosen words beat a paragraph.
3. **The "could have written X, instead Y" move**, done on a sentence Collins never wrote:
   *"A past-tense Katniss would have written…"* Inventing the ordinary version is the fastest
   proof that the real version was a choice.
4. **The cost is named and it actually hurts** — *"grief but not perspective."* A technique
   with no cost is a technique you haven't looked at hard enough.
5. **The verdict connects the technique to the book's meaning** — a book about a live
   broadcast, narrated live. The last line does new work instead of repeating the first one.
