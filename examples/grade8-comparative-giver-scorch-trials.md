## 15 — Comparative Essay *(Tier 3 · built over 4 days)*

**Both *The Giver* and *The Scorch Trials* put children inside an experiment run by adults who
say it is for everyone's good. The difference is who gets told the reason — and Lowry and
Dashner spend that decision on completely different things.**

Lowry keeps the reason away from the people it is being done to. Jonas's Community has an
explanation for everything it does, but the explanation is never actually said out loud to
anyone; it has been replaced by vocabulary. Nobody in the Community lies. They say **"release"**
and they say **"precision of language"** while using the least precise word in the book, and the
result is a whole town that never has to know what it agreed to. The one time the reasoning does
get spoken, it is Jonas himself saying it, secondhand, having just been handed the memories:
**"We really have to protect people from wrong choices"** (Lowry, ch. 13). He is repeating a
sentence the Community has never had to defend, because there is nobody left who could argue
back. That is what the silence buys Lowry — the reader learns what release means at the same
moment Jonas does, and the book gets one enormous detonation out of a word we had been reading
for eighteen chapters.

Dashner does the opposite, and he does it immediately. WICKED explains itself constantly. It
announces Phase Two, it names the deadline and the distance, it tells the Gladers there is a
cure and that they are being measured for it, and when it wants to be really clear it tattoos
the purpose onto their necks — Teresa's says **"The Betrayer"** before she has betrayed anyone.
The organization's motto is a three-word argument, **"WICKED is good,"** repeated until it stops
sounding like a claim and starts sounding like a name. Where Lowry withholds the reason so the
reveal can land, Dashner hands the reason over on the first day and it changes nothing: Thomas
knows he is a subject, knows he is being watched, knows there is a plan — and crosses the Scorch
anyway. Knowing the reason turns out to be no protection at all.

This comparison strains in one place, and the strain is the interesting part: Lowry is writing a
quiet book about one town and Dashner is writing a chase, so of course one can afford eighteen
chapters of silence and the other cannot. But the strain exposes the trade. Silence is a
resource you can spend exactly once. Lowry spends it beautifully and *The Giver* is over almost
as soon as she does. Dashner gives his up on page one, and what he buys with it is a book that
can spend four hundred pages on a harder question than *what is really going on* — namely,
*whose reason is good enough*, which is a question Jonas's Community is never allowed to ask.

If I have to pick, Lowry wrote the better single moment and Dashner wrote the more useful book.
The thing I can't settle is whether that is a fair fight, or whether *The Scorch Trials* only
gets to ask its question because *The Giver* already taught a generation of readers to be
suspicious of a calm adult with an explanation (Lowry, chs. 13–19; Dashner, chs. 1–10).
