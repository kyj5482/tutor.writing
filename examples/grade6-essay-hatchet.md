# Grade 6 Example — 12 Essay, second book: *Hatchet*

The second **12 Essay** example for grade 6, so the template that a ladder goal makes you
write most weeks doesn't always arrive on the same book. Pair with `grade6-growth.md`
(*Percy Jackson*) and alternate.

Same reader as `grade6-hatchet.md`: a 6th grader who has read **chapters 1–3** — Brian
Robeson, 13, is flying to his dad in Canada with his parents' divorce and "The Secret" on
his mind; the pilot has a heart attack; Brian crash-lands the plane into a lake.

**Written at Tier 2**, and built **one paragraph a day over 3 days** — that is the only
reason it is this long. Good, not perfect. Yours should sound like YOU.

---

## 12 — Essay *(Tier 2 · built over 3 days)*

**Brian's first enemy isn't the wilderness. It's his own head, and the plane crash is
where he starts to win that fight.**

The first reason I think this is the way the author writes the moment the pilot dies. He
writes, **"He was alone. In the roaring plane with no pilot he was alone. Alone."** The
word *alone* comes three times in a row and the sentences get shorter each time, so
reading it feels like panic sounds. Nothing has attacked Brian yet. There is no bear and
no starvation — there is just a boy in a seat with his own thoughts getting louder, and
Paulsen makes us hear them before he makes us watch Brian survive anything.

The second reason is what Brian does with the radio. He tries it, he loses the signal, and
then he has to decide something no thirteen-year-old should have to decide: nobody is
coming. That is the moment the book actually turns. He stops looking for a grown-up to fix
it and starts flying the plane himself, badly, toward water because water is softer than
trees. Deciding that took more courage than the crash did, because the crash happened to
him and the decision didn't.

So the hatchet on his belt — the present he was embarrassed to wear in the city — is not
really what saves Brian. What saves him is that in three chapters he goes from a kid
waiting for help to a kid who answers his own question. That is why Paulsen spends so long
inside Brian's thoughts before the survival part starts: he wants us to know exactly what
Brian sounded like *before*, so we can hear the difference later.

*(≈250 words · Day 1 = the thesis + paragraph 1 · Day 2 = paragraph 2 · Day 3 = the ending
and the read-aloud fixes.)*

---

### What to steal from this one

1. **The thesis argues with the obvious answer.** "The wilderness is the enemy" is what
   anybody would say. Saying *"his own head is the enemy"* gives the essay something to
   prove — and a thesis nobody could disagree with isn't a thesis, it's a summary.
2. **The quote is inside the sentence, not dropped on top of it.** *He writes, "He was
   alone…"* — then the very next sentence says what the quote **does** (three times, shorter
   each time, panic). Evidence with no explanation is just decoration.
3. **The ending adds instead of repeating.** It doesn't say "so that's why Brian's head is
   the enemy." It says *why the author built the book this way* — one new thought, in the
   last three lines.
