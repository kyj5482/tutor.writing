# Grade 6 Examples — *A Wrinkle in Time* by Madeleine L'Engle

All examples below imagine a 6th grader who read **chapters 1–4**: Meg Murry is awkward,
angry, and in trouble at school. Her scientist father disappeared over a year ago doing
secret government work, and people in town whisper that he ran off. Her five-year-old
brother Charles Wallace barely talks to strangers but seems to read Meg's mind. On a stormy
night, a strange old woman named Mrs Whatsit blows in, eats their sandwiches, and mentions
on her way out that "there is such a thing as a tesseract" — which makes Meg's mother go
white. The next day Meg and Charles Wallace meet Calvin O'Keefe, and with Mrs Who and Mrs
Which they "tesser" — fold space — to a planet called Uriel, where they are shown a
terrible dark shadow spreading across the universe.

Examples are written at **Tier 2**, with one marked Tier 3. They're good, not perfect —
your writing should sound like YOU.

**👀 Notice in every example:** the quote is in the **main writing**, not saved for the
bonus round.

---

## 01 — Summary

Today I read the first four chapters of *A Wrinkle in Time*, where a normal stormy night
turns into something impossible. First, Meg can't sleep because of the storm and because
she got in trouble at school again, and she goes downstairs to find Charles Wallace already
making her cocoa, like he knew she was coming. After that, a strange old woman called Mrs
Whatsit shows up soaking wet in the middle of the night, and Charles Wallace acts like he
already knows her. Then, right before she leaves, Mrs Whatsit says "there is such a thing as
a tesseract," and Meg's mother goes completely white and almost drops something. Finally,
the next day Meg and Charles Wallace meet Calvin, and the three Mrs W's take them off Earth
to a planet called Uriel. Now they have seen a huge dark shadow in space, and they have been
told their father is somewhere behind it.

## 02 — Prediction

I predict that the dark shadow is the thing holding Meg's father prisoner, and that Meg is
going to be the one who gets him out, not Charles Wallace. One clue is that the Mrs W's keep
telling Meg her faults will help her, which is a strange thing to say unless her stubbornness
is going to matter later. Another clue is that when the children see the Dark Thing, Mrs
Whatsit doesn't explain it — the author is saving it, and authors save the thing the ending
needs. My confidence is 7 out of 10, because Charles Wallace is the one with the powers, so
part of me thinks he'll be the hero instead.

## 03 — Character Diary

Dear Diary,
Everyone keeps telling me to be patient. Be patient, Meg. Give it time, Meg. Your father
will come home, Meg.
Today Mr. Jenkins told me I should "face facts" about my father, and I got so angry my ears
were ringing. I know what people say in town. I know what they think he did. The worst part
isn't even that they say it — it's that I don't have one single thing to say back, because I
don't know where he is either.
Then a woman in a pink stole ate our sandwiches at two in the morning and said a word that
made Mother turn to stone. *Tesseract.* Mother wouldn't explain it. Nobody ever explains
anything to me. But she knew it. I watched her face and she *knew* it.
So something is happening. Finally, something is happening.
— Meg

## 04 — Opinion / Response

I think the town is being unfair to Meg, but Meg is also making it worse for herself. My
evidence is that when people say things about her father, Meg gets in fights instead of
walking away, and even the principal points out that she is "not making things easy" for
herself. This shows that Meg is defending her dad in the only way she knows, but hitting
people doesn't make anyone believe her. That's why I feel bad for her and also want to shake
her a little — she is right about her father and wrong about how to prove it.

## 05 — Letter to a Character

Dear Meg,
When Mr. Jenkins told you to face facts about your father, I actually got mad reading it.
Adults do that thing where they call giving up "being realistic."
I think you should listen to Charles Wallace more, even when he sounds too old for five. He
notices things about you that you don't notice about yourself.
I have two questions for you: Do you actually believe your father is alive, or do you just
need to? And why do you think Mrs Whatsit said the word *tesseract* out loud in front of
your mother, instead of quietly?
Also — be careful. I know something you don't. The thing the Mrs W's are protecting you from
isn't a person.
Your friend, Mina

## 06 — Rewrite the Scene (What If…?)

In the book, Mrs Whatsit says the word "tesseract" and then leaves before Mrs. Murry can
react. But what if Meg's mother had run after her?

Mrs. Murry got to the door before it finished closing.
"Say that word again."
Mrs Whatsit turned around in the rain, and she did not look like a funny old woman anymore.
"You heard it perfectly well the first time."
"Where did you hear it?" Mrs. Murry's voice cracked. "That word is not in any paper. It is
not in *anything*. My husband and I were the only —"
"Yes," Mrs Whatsit said gently. "You were."
Behind them, Charles Wallace stood in the kitchen doorway with his cocoa, not surprised at
all. Meg looked at her brother, then at her mother's face, and understood that she was the
only person in the room who didn't already know.

## 07 — Golden Line *(Tier 3 — with a word zoom-in)*

**My golden line:** "Wild nights are my glory!" — Mrs Whatsit (chapter 1)

In the story, Mrs Whatsit says this after blowing into the Murrys' kitchen soaking wet, in
the middle of a storm that has scared everyone else in the house. I think the author wrote
it this way to tell us instantly that Mrs Whatsit is not what she looks like. A lost old
woman would say "what a terrible night." Only something powerful calls a storm *glorious*.

The word I keep looking at is "glory." It's a huge word — it belongs to kings and to
heavens, not to weather, and definitely not to an old lady in stolen boots. Putting a giant
word in a tiny person's mouth is the whole trick of this character. It makes me wonder what
Mrs Whatsit actually is, because whatever she is, she isn't afraid of the dark.

## 08 — Connection

In the book, Meg feels like everyone in her family fits somewhere except her — her mother is
beautiful and brilliant, the twins are normal and good at sports, and Charles Wallace is a
genius. She is the one who is "the odd one out." This reminds me of when my cousins all
learned to swim the same summer and I was the only one still in the shallow end, and the
worst part wasn't the swimming, it was everyone being nice about it. They're alike because
being the one who doesn't fit is loneliest when nobody is being mean to you. But they're
different because I caught up by the next summer, and Meg's problem isn't something she can
practice her way out of. I understand Meg better now — when she snaps at people, it's not
because she's mean, it's because she is tired of being the one everybody is patient with.

## 09 — Quick Write 3-2-1 *(for a short-reading day)*

**3 things that happened:**
1. Meg couldn't sleep during the storm and went down to find Charles Wallace already awake.
2. Mrs Whatsit arrived soaking wet in the middle of the night and ate their sandwiches.
3. On her way out she said "there is such a thing as a tesseract," and Mrs. Murry went white.

**2 words:** "Tesseract" — nobody explains it, but Mrs. Murry reacted like it was a secret,
so I think it's a way of traveling that scientists only had a theory about. "Sport" — Meg
says her father called Charles Wallace a "sport," and from how it's used I think it means
something rare that happens in nature, not a game.

**1 question:** Why did Mrs Whatsit say the word out loud instead of just leaving? My guess
is that she wanted Mrs. Murry to know that she knows — like it was a message, not an
accident.

## 10 — Book Review *(written as if the whole book was finished)*

What if the thing you hate most about yourself turned out to be the only weapon that works?
*A Wrinkle in Time* by Madeleine L'Engle is about Meg Murry, who travels across the universe
with her brother and her friend Calvin to find her missing scientist father, and ends up
facing a darkness that is swallowing entire planets.

The best thing about this book is that Meg doesn't win by becoming brave or nice or good at
school. She wins with her anger and her stubbornness — the exact things adults keep telling
her to fix. There's also a planet where every child bounces a ball in perfect rhythm, and it
is genuinely one of the creepiest things I have ever read.

One thing that could be better is that some of the explanations about tessering are hard to
follow, and I had to reread pages more than once. I give it 4.5 out of 5 stars — it loses
half a star for the confusing parts, but it earns everything else because the ending made me
put the book down and just sit there. If you liked *Percy Jackson* but want something
stranger and quieter, read this one.

## 11 — ACE Write (Bonus) *(tutor asked: "Why does the author make Charles Wallace so strange?")*

**A —** I think the author makes Charles Wallace strange so that we believe impossible things
are possible before any impossible thing actually happens.

**C —** In the book, Charles Wallace is already making cocoa when Meg comes downstairs,
because he says he knew she'd come, and later he tells her, "I understand you." He also knows
Mrs Whatsit before anyone introduces them.

**E —** This shows that the magic starts inside the family before it starts in space. By the
time the Mrs W's fold the universe, the reader has already accepted that a five-year-old can
read minds, so we don't fight it. It also makes Meg feel more alone, because even her little
brother is in on something she isn't.
